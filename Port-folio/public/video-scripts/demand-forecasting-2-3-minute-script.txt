Shipments and demand are not the same thing. Shipments show what stores ordered and what the supply chain was able to deliver. Point-of-sale data shows what customers actually bought. When planning uses only shipments, it can react late to a product that is gaining or losing demand. That distinction changes what the forecast needs to learn. That was the problem addressed in this project. The documented planning process relied on shipped-order history, but shipments lag consumer sell-through and can be distorted by available inventory. That gap can create overstock on products that are fading and stockouts on products that are rising. I helped build a two-signal demand-forecasting approach. The first input was point-of-sale history, representing actual consumer purchases. The second was shipment history, representing store orders and the supply response. Instead of treating the signals as substitutes, the model learned from both, including the divergence between them. I engineered lag and rolling-window features per product to represent recent movement, trend, and seasonality. Machine-learning models then produced upcoming product-level demand forecasts. Before a forecast reached planners, it was backtested on held-out time periods. That separation mattered because a forecast should demonstrate value on future-like data, not on the same periods used for training. The output also landed directly in the planning workflow rather than remaining in a separate analytical report. This was a cross-functional delivery with data scientists and business teams. My role spanned data engineering and analysis: preparing the two input signals, shaping time-based features, supporting the forecasting workflow, and connecting its output to planning for upcoming sales orders. The documented result was a fifteen-percent improvement in forecast accuracy compared with the prior baseline. The portfolio does not record the exact algorithm, forecast horizon, error measure, or definition of that baseline, so I keep those details explicit as unknowns rather than fill them in. What is verified is the design and outcome: combining actual sell-through with shipment history, validating across held-out periods, and operationalizing product-level forecasts gave planning a more informed view of upcoming demand than shipments alone.
