A crop can look healthy from a satellite and still underperform because the soil or the season tells a different story. That is the central problem behind this crop-yield forecasting project: no single signal captures the complete system. Vegetation indices describe what is visible above the ground. Soil properties describe nutrients, pH, and moisture. Weather describes rainfall, temperature, and growing-degree days. Historical yield provides a useful baseline. I structured the documented solution by bringing those signals into one feature space. Satellite NDVI and EVI trajectories, soil measurements, seasonal weather, and prior-season yield are aligned to the crop calendar. Instead of treating calendar months as interchangeable, the feature windows follow growth stages, so each measurement is compared in the context of what the crop is doing at that point in the season. The modeling approach uses an ensemble of Random Forest and XGBoost trained on historical yield. The reason for combining models is to avoid relying on a single learner when vegetation and weather errors may behave differently. The output is a field-level yield estimate rather than a broad regional average. Validation was a critical part of the design. I used season-based cross-validation so the model was scored on seasons it did not train on. Predictions were compared with actual yield using R-squared and root mean squared error. I also used feature-importance analysis to identify which inputs had the strongest relationship with the forecast, making the result easier to inspect than a prediction alone. The completed project demonstrates an end-to-end forecasting method: gather complementary signals, align them to crop development, build a fused feature set, train an ensemble, and evaluate honestly on unseen seasons. One limitation is important to state. The portfolio catalog describes this as the standard architecture and doesn't record the exact dataset, crop, geography, or final R-squared and RMSE values. So I present the verified method and evaluation design without inventing a performance percentage. The value of the project is the disciplined combination of agronomic signals and time-aware validation to produce an earlier, field-level view of expected yield.
